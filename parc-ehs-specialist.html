How I protect myself and the crew

Process
  • Do not guess. Respirator, monitoring, asbestos, waste profile, permit — pause and ask the Director or the assigned competent person.
  • Facts, not opinions. “Damaged ladder removed from service 9:20 a.m.; supervisor notified; replacement requested.” Not “everyone was reckless.”

2. Injury, illness, and the Cal/OSHA clock

Care, notify, facts

Care comes first. Pause related work and notify the supervisor and EHS Director immediately; notify HR through PARC’s injury-reporting process. A fatality or potentially serious work-related injury or illness may trigger an immediate Cal/OSHA reporting obligation, with an outside limit of eight hours for the employer. I preserve the facts and escalate right away — I do not wait, diagnose, or make the reportability call alone.

“Immediately” means as soon as practically possible, but no later than eight hours after the employer knew or with diligent inquiry would have known. A serious injury or illness includes inpatient hospitalization other than observation or diagnostic testing, amputation, loss of an eye, or serious permanent disfigurement. The company makes the reporting determination. My job is to get care started, control the immediate hazard, notify the Director, supervisor, and PARC’s internal reporting contacts, and preserve facts. I do not personally report to Cal/OSHA unless PARC assigns that function. I do not wait until Monday. I do not decide “that wasn’t serious” by myself. A clinic visit is not automatically this clock. A near miss is not this clock unless it also meets the employer’s serious-injury criteria.

PARC owns its Cal/OSHA recordkeeping program. If assigned, I will gather complete facts and support accurate, timely entry and review; I will not independently decide a recordability question beyond my authority. Work-related injuries and illnesses are generally recordable when they result in death, days away, restricted work or transfer, medical treatment beyond first aid, loss of consciousness, or a significant injury or illness diagnosed by a licensed health-care professional. Workers’ compensation is HR and the carrier. I keep facts consistent across packets. I do not mix blame into a claim form.

5. ISN (ISNetworld) and Avetta

Customer gate — not OSHA

ISN is the customer’s contractor-compliance gate: current records help the crew get on site; inaccurate records create safety, business, and liability problems.

ISN usually means ISNetworld — a private online contractor-management and prequalification platform. Big customers use it to decide whether a contractor like PARC has current safety, insurance, training, and compliance documents before allowing that contractor’s crews onto a jobsite. It is not OSHA, Cal/OSHA, or a government license. Avetta is a similar private customer gate. The posting lists both. I have not administered either yet.

Before PARC can work for a refinery, utility, manufacturer, government facility, large construction owner, or other major client, that client may require PARC to show evidence that its business and workers meet that client’s requirements. If required documents are expired, missing, inaccurate, or not accepted, the client can flag PARC’s account — or prevent the crew from mobilizing.

ISN is a contractor prequalification and compliance platform that customers use to verify whether contractors meet their safety, insurance, training, and documentation requirements before crews work on site. For this role, I would learn PARC’s account ownership, customer-specific requirements, document-approval process, and expiration calendar. My job would be to help make sure Sacramento records are current and accurate before mobilization. I understand that an upload is not automatically an approval, and I would never submit a document I cannot verify.

RAVS (Review and Verification Services) is ISN’s document-review service. Reviewers read the written programs you upload and check them against regulatory elements and the particular hiring client’s checklist. Missing language comes back as a deficiency. You revise, the authorized owner resubmits, and you wait for review. Uploaded is not approved. Generic templates and implied-but-unwritten controls fail. I do not rewrite PARC’s written programs to clear RAVS unless the Director owns that change. I collect the true document, flag the deficiency, and route it.

ISN vs Avetta: both are private customer gates, not government licenses. ISN is known for a more standardized written-program review (RAVS) and a live contractor grade that hiring clients use. Avetta is similar in purpose but requirements and scoring are often more client-specific questionnaires. A company can be on both. Clearing one does not clear the other. I learn whichever PARC actually uses for Sacramento customers.

Why accounts get flagged or blocked: expired insurance; stale 300A; written program past review date or RAVS deficiency not closed; expired training or competent-person cards; questionnaire unanswered; company-name mismatch; citation or incident with no corrective-action evidence; EMR or injury-rate above a client’s threshold; assuming “uploaded” meant “accepted.” I hunt those before mobilization. I do not hide an injury or backdate a program to clear a score.

EMR: that number comes from workers’ compensation claims history, not from clicking upload. I do not “improve EMR” in the portal. Over time it improves when fewer and less severe claims occur. My contribution is real field controls, accurate incident facts, closed corrective actions, and never concealing an injury. HR, the carrier, and the Director own the EMR letter. I transmit an approved letter if assigned.

I am not the executive owner of PARC’s ISN account on day one. Show me the account, the customers, and the weekly review time. I keep Sacramento records current and accurate. I do not fake the file.
